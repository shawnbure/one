# Company-Model Reality Check — Services Firm, Not (Yet) a Product Company

**Status:** Course-correction (supersedes the implicit "we're a product company" assumption in the other docs)
**Prompted by:** the founder's own gut-check — "did I jump from AI consulting straight to a greenfield product when I'm not a product company?"
**Answer:** Partly yes. The pattern-spotting was right; the leap to a vertically-integrated hardware/OS/marketplace product was ~4 steps too far for a services firm. Right-size below.

---

## 1. The honest gap

workrr is an **AI consulting / services** firm. The appliance platform we designed is a **fundable, capital-intensive, hardware product company** — near-opposite on capital, cash flow, core skills, timeline, and risk. Moving from one to the other is founding a different company, not evolving the current one. The gut-check is correct.

What *wasn't* overreach: noticing the repeated pattern ("every client engagement requires understanding the business" — Phase 1 of the original vision). That repeated, expensive thing is exactly the seed a product can grow from. The overreach was jumping from "I keep rebuilding this" straight to "build an appliance + OS + marketplace."

## 2. Two moves that right-size it

**A. Decouple the software value from the hardware bet.**
The moat (the catalog/BKL) is *software*. The appliance is just packaging. Prove the whole value — "one private, connected, sourced model of the business" — by deploying the **software on a client's own server** inside an engagement you already bill. No OEM, no fleet/OTA, no inventory, no capital. The appliance is a later packaging decision, earned only if demand is real. Strip every capital-intensive part (hardware, OS, marketplace) until something pulls.

**B. Build it as a services accelerator, funded by client work.**
Next client who hires workrr to "make AI work over our data" → build the catalog as workrr's **reusable delivery tool**. It makes the consulting faster/better immediately — real, paid ROI on day one. Then watch for the tell:

> A client asks: *"Can we keep this? Can we pay to keep using it?"*

**That sentence is the moment a product company is actually born** — funded by revenue, validated by real usage, no speculative hardware. Strongest possible position.

## 3. What this changes about validation

workrr already has the asset the smoke test ignores: **paying clients with this exact pain, and the relationship to ask them.** A landing page tests a *message*; a client saying "deploy that for us next quarter" tests the *business*. Do the smoke test (it's cheap and clarifies the pitch), but treat existing-client conversations — and ideally one paid pilot of the catalog-as-accelerator — as the higher-signal validation.

## 4. Status of the other docs

Not wasted — they're the **product thesis to reach for IF the accelerator shows standalone pull.** But they assume a product company. Until demand is proven, treat them as a *someday blueprint*, not a *build plan*. The near-term plan is: productize the repeatable catalog for existing clients; let billable work fund it; watch for pull.

## 5. Near-term (next ~6 months) — services-shaped, not startup-shaped
1. Run the smoke test (message clarity + cheap signal).
2. In parallel, talk to existing clients about this pain; look for one willing to pilot.
3. Build the **minimum catalog** as an internal accelerator, deployed on a client's infra (software only, no appliance).
4. Deliver a paying engagement with it; measure whether it makes delivery better and whether the client wants to *keep* it.
5. Only if clients pull ("we want to buy this") → revisit the product/appliance blueprint and the funding/team that requires.

**One-line rule:** don't become a hardware company to find out if the software is wanted. Prove the software with clients first; package it only when they're pulling it out of your hands.
